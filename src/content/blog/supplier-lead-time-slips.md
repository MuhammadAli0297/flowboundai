A supplier's lead time rarely blows out all at once. It creeps: two weeks becomes three, three becomes
four, and by the time it's five, you've already placed a purchase order assuming the old number. The
stockout that follows feels sudden, but the actual cause was weeks old and nobody was watching for it.

## The lead time creep nobody notices until it's a stockout

Most small teams track lead time the way they learned it once, from an old email or a conversation with a
sales rep, and never update it unless something goes badly wrong. Suppliers, meanwhile, deal with their own
capacity issues, material shortages, and shipping delays, and rarely proactively tell you their timelines
have moved. You find out when your order is late, which is the worst possible time to find out.

## Three signs a supplier relationship needs attention

- Recent purchase orders are consistently arriving later than the lead time you have on file.
- Order confirmations have gotten vaguer, a date range instead of a firm date, or no date at all.
- You've had to follow up more than once on the same order to get a status update.

Any one of these on its own might be nothing. Two or more together usually means the lead time you're
planning against is already wrong.

## What to do the moment a lead time changes

Update the number everywhere you use it, not just in your head. If your reorder point math assumes a
two-week lead time and the real number is now four, every reorder placed against the old assumption is
already running behind. Recalculate your reorder point with the new lead time, and if the gap is big
enough, consider whether this order needs to go out today instead of waiting for the usual trigger.
