A supplier's cost goes up, or demand for a SKU shifts, and the sell price should move to match. In most
small operations, it eventually does, once someone notices and gets around to updating it. The gap between
when the cost changed and when the price finally caught up is pure margin leakage, and it's almost never
tracked as its own line item, so nobody sees how much it actually adds up to.

## The gap between a cost change and a price change

Prices don't drift out of date all at once. A supplier's cost ticks up a few percent, demand for a popular
SKU climbs enough that it could bear a higher price, and the sell price just sits where it was because
repricing isn't anyone's daily job. It's a task that competes with everything else and usually loses.

## Why repricing usually waits for someone to notice

Repricing requires noticing the underlying shift first, which means someone has to be watching supplier
costs and demand closely enough to catch the moment a price should move. Most small teams check pricing
periodically, not continuously, which means the gap between "should have repriced" and "actually repriced"
is measured in weeks, not hours.

## What that lag actually costs over time

A percent or two of margin on a single order looks trivial. The same gap, repeated across every order on
that SKU for the weeks it takes someone to notice and update the price, adds up to a real number by the end
of a quarter, one that never shows up as a single expense, just a slightly lower margin than it should have
been.

## Adjusting price the moment the input changes

The fix isn't reviewing prices more often. It's not needing a review at all for the routine cases.
[Pricing](/pricing) adjusts your sell price within the bounds you set the moment a supplier cost or demand
signal actually changes, so margin holds without anyone having to re-run the math every time something
shifts.
