Shipping damage almost never comes from one bad box. It comes from a packaging standard that was never
actually written down, a service level picked for cost instead of the product's fragility, and a claims
process too slow to catch the pattern before it repeats on the next truck. Fix the first two and damage
drops. Fix the third and the damage that still happens stops costing you money on top of the replacement.

## Why freight arrives damaged

Three causes account for most of it, and they're not equally common.

**Under-packaging** is the biggest one. A box sized right for the product but with no void fill lets the
item shift and slam into the corners every time the pallet gets moved, and a pallet gets moved eight to
twelve times between your dock and the customer's, forklift to truck, truck to hub, hub to truck again,
truck to final delivery. Void fill isn't padding for padding's sake, it's the difference between an item
riding still and an item riding loose for a thousand miles.

**Wrong carrier or service level for the product** is the second cause, and it's the one that's easiest to
miss because the shipment usually arrives on time. A parcel carrier's automated sorting belts are built for
boxes that can survive a 3-foot drop and a hard corner impact, because that's literally what happens on a
high-speed sort line. A palletized LTL shipment moves differently, freight gets reshuffled by hand or
forklift at every terminal it transfers through, and a carrier with a reputation for a high damage
frequency on a specific lane will keep producing it regardless of how well you packed the box, because the
handling itself is the problem, not the packaging. If you're still choosing carriers on rate alone, the
[carrier selection framework](/blog/cost-of-picking-the-wrong-carrier/) is worth reading before the
packaging fixes below, since the best packaging standard in the world can't fully offset a carrier that
handles freight roughly on a given lane.

**Mishandling at transfer points** is the third, and it's the one you can't packaging your way out of
entirely. Every terminal transfer is a chance for a pallet to get dropped, crushed under something heavier,
or stacked wrong. You can't watch every dock, but you can build packaging that survives a bad handoff
instead of one that only survives a gentle one, and you can watch which lanes and carriers produce damage at
a higher rate than others, which is exactly the signal the last section of this post covers.

## Build a packaging standard by weight and fragility class, not by product

Most small distributors pack the way whoever's on the floor that day learned to pack, which means the same
SKU gets boxed differently depending on who's working. A written standard fixes that, and it doesn't need to
be complicated. Sort your catalog into three or four fragility classes and set a rule per class instead of
per SKU:

- **Class 1, durable and heavy** (cast metal parts, solid wood components, anything over roughly 40 lbs with
  no fragile surface): single-wall corrugated is fine, minimal void fill, focus spend on a strong pallet and
  proper strapping instead of the box itself.
- **Class 2, moderate** (packaged consumer goods, most wholesale dry goods, boxed hardware): double-wall
  corrugated, void fill on all sides, corner reinforcement if the box is going LTL rather than parcel.
- **Class 3, fragile** (glass, ceramics, anything with a finished surface that shows a scratch, electronics
  without their own rigid housing): double-wall or a foam-lined insert, a 2-inch minimum cushion on every
  side, and a "this side up" plus fragile marking that's actually visible, not printed small in a corner.
- **Class 4, fragile and heavy** (the combination that breaks the most boxes, think a 60 lb ceramic fixture
  or a glass-fronted display case): custom crating or a reinforced double-wall box rated for the actual
  weight, never a box sized for a lighter item and reinforced with extra tape, since tape doesn't add
  load-bearing strength, it just holds a weak box shut a little longer.

The point of sorting by class instead of by individual SKU is that a new product gets a packaging rule the
moment it's added to the catalog, not after the first damage claim teaches you what should have been
obvious. Write the four rules down somewhere the whole floor can see them, a laminated sheet at the packing
station works better than a policy document nobody opens.

One thing worth testing before you commit to a class assignment: drop-test a sample box from about three
feet onto a hard corner, the same shock a sort-line drop delivers, and open it. If anything inside shifted
more than an inch, the void fill isn't doing its job regardless of what class you assigned it. This costs an
afternoon and a few boxes and it's the cheapest fix you'll make all quarter, since a packaging standard that
looks right on paper but fails a real drop test is worse than no standard, it gives you false confidence.

## Match the service level to the class, not just the destination

Once products are sorted by fragility class, the shipping decision changes too. A Class 3 or 4 item going
parcel through a high-speed automated sort facility is taking on damage risk a Class 1 item never sees on
the same route, and the fix isn't always "spend more," it's matching the mode to what the product can
actually survive. Sometimes that means paying for a freight service with hand-loaded, guaranteed handling
instead of a cheaper parcel rate for something fragile enough that the sort belt itself is the risk. This is
the same trade-off [shipping optimization](/shipping-optimization/) is built around: comparing carriers and
lanes on total delivered outcome, not sticker rate, so a fragile SKU doesn't end up on the cheapest option
that also happens to have the highest damage frequency for that class of freight.

## Filing a claim: what actually gets you paid

Damage prevention gets you most of the way, but even a good packaging standard won't get you to zero, and
how you handle the claim decides whether you eat the loss or recover it.

**Inspect before signing, not after the driver leaves.** Signing a clean proof of delivery is you telling
the carrier the freight arrived in good condition. If a pallet looks off, wrapped stretch film torn, a
corner crushed, note it on the delivery receipt before you sign, in the driver's presence. "Clean POD, filed
a claim three days later" is the single most common reason a legitimate claim gets denied, because the
paperwork says you already confirmed it arrived fine.

**Document before you touch anything.** Photograph the outside of the box or pallet as it sits, the damage
itself, and the packaging materials inside, void fill included, before repacking or moving anything. A
carrier's claims adjuster is looking for evidence the packaging was adequate for the product, and a photo of
crushed void fill next to an intact product tells a very different story than a photo of a bare item rattling
in an oversized box.

**Describe the damage, don't assign blame, in the paperwork itself.** "2-inch crush to bottom left corner,
contents shifted" gets processed. "Your driver clearly dropped this" gets read as an assumption, not a fact,
and can slow a claim down or give the carrier grounds to dispute it. Save your carrier-performance opinion
for the scorecard conversation, not the claim form.

**File fast and keep the damaged goods.** Most carriers want notice within 5 business days, and while formal
claims windows can run months, a claim filed the same week has a materially better track record than one
filed weeks later once the details are fuzzy. Don't discard the damaged product either, a carrier can request
it for inspection before paying out, and disposing of it early is grounds to deny the claim outright.

**Track claims like you'd track anything else that costs money**, not in a folder of emails. A simple log,
SKU, carrier, lane, dollar amount, filed date, resolution date, turns "we had some damage last quarter" into
a real number you can act on. Without that log, a slow-paying carrier or a chronically damage-prone lane
just looks like bad luck instead of a pattern, and it's the pattern that's actually worth fixing.

## A rising damage rate on one lane is a signal, not noise

Here's the part most packaging advice skips entirely: individual damage claims are a cost problem, but a
damage *rate* that's climbing on one specific lane or with one specific carrier is an early warning that
something changed upstream, a new terminal handling that route, a carrier's service quality slipping, a
seasonal volume surge overwhelming a hub. Treating each claim as an isolated event means you only notice the
pattern once it's expensive enough to be obvious, usually a quarter or two after it started.

The fix is the same discipline you'd apply to a defect rate on a physical product: track damage as a rate
per lane and per carrier, not just a running total, and watch for the rate moving in the wrong direction
before it becomes [a genuinely growing problem](/blog/one-off-defect-or-growing-problem/) instead of a
one-off. A single damaged pallet from an otherwise-reliable carrier is normal freight variance. Three damaged
pallets from the same carrier on the same lane in a month is a signal worth a conversation, maybe a service
level change, maybe moving that lane's volume elsewhere. This is exactly what
[Quality Monitoring](/quality-monitoring/) is built to catch, treating damage and defect patterns as a
running signal across returns and inspections instead of something that only gets noticed when someone
happens to compare two months of claims side by side.

## Where Flowbound fits

Reducing shipping damage is really two separate jobs that happen to feed each other. One is picking the
right carrier and service level for the freight in the first place, which is what
[Shipping Optimization](/shipping-optimization/) handles: comparing carriers and lanes automatically and
recommending the option suited to the shipment, not just the cheapest one on paper. The other is catching a
damage pattern early enough to act on it, which is what [Quality Monitoring](/quality-monitoring/) handles,
flagging a rising damage or defect rate as it shows up in returns and inspections instead of waiting for
someone to notice the trend by hand. Run separately, each one helps. Run together, a rising damage rate on a
lane can actually change which carrier gets picked for the next shipment on that lane, instead of the two
problems living in different spreadsheets that never talk to each other.

## FAQ

**What's the most common cause of shipping damage?**
Under-packaging, specifically missing or inadequate void fill that lets the product shift and absorb impact
during the eight to twelve handling touches a typical shipment goes through. Wrong carrier or service level
for the product's fragility class is the second most common cause, which is why [carrier
selection](/blog/cost-of-picking-the-wrong-carrier/) and packaging need to be solved together, not treated
as separate problems.

**How long do I have to file a freight damage claim?**
Report the damage to the carrier within 5 business days if possible, and file the formal claim as soon as
you have documentation, even though most carriers' rules allow up to nine months. A claim filed the same
week the damage is discovered has a meaningfully better track record than one filed weeks later, and
[Quality Monitoring](/quality-monitoring/) helps by flagging damage as it's logged instead of it sitting
unreported in a returns pile.

**Should I sign for a delivery if the packaging looks damaged?**
Note the damage on the delivery receipt before you sign, in the driver's presence, rather than signing clean
and filing a claim afterward. A clean proof of delivery tells the carrier you confirmed the shipment arrived
in good condition, and it's the single most common reason a legitimate claim gets denied.

**How do I know if a carrier has a damage problem versus normal bad luck?**
Track damage as a rate per carrier and per lane, not a running total. A single damaged pallet from an
otherwise-reliable carrier is normal variance. Several damaged pallets from the same carrier on the same
lane within a month or two is a pattern worth acting on, the same logic [Shipping
Optimization](/shipping-optimization/) applies when comparing carriers for a lane going forward.

**Does better packaging cost more than the damage it prevents?**
Usually not once you account for the full cost of a claim: the replacement product, the freight to resend
it, the customer service time, and the customer relationship if the replacement is also late. Sorting
products into a few fragility classes and setting one packaging rule per class, rather than upgrading every
box, keeps the added material cost targeted at the shipments that actually need it.
