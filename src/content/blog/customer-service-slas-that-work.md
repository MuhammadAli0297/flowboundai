A customer service SLA is a written promise about how fast you'll respond to a question and how fast you'll
actually resolve it, broken out by channel because email and chat don't run on the same clock. The number
that matters isn't the fastest time you're capable of on a good day. It's the time you can hit on a bad one,
with someone out sick and forty tickets already in the queue, because that's the day the SLA actually gets
tested.

Most small distributors either skip the SLA entirely (support runs on vibes, and every customer gets a
different experience depending on who's covering the inbox that day) or copy one off a template built for a
company with a 20-person shift-covered support floor. Both fail the same way: nobody actually knows what to
expect, including the team answering the tickets.

## Why the big-company SLA backfires on a small team

The advice that circulates most, respond within one hour, 24/7, across every channel, comes from companies
staffing multiple shifts across time zones. A wholesale distributor with three people who also pick orders,
answer supplier calls, and handle the phone doesn't have that coverage, and posting a promise that assumes
it creates two specific problems.

First, it breaks the moment reality doesn't cooperate. One person calls in sick, or a supplier issue eats
the morning, and the one-hour promise turns into three hours on a normal Tuesday. A customer holding a
screenshot of your stated SLA next to your actual reply time doesn't read that as "a busy day," they read it
as "this company doesn't keep its word." A missed SLA that was never realistic to begin with does more
damage to trust than never publishing one.

Second, an unrealistic response-time target quietly trains the team to answer fast instead of answer right.
When the clock is the thing being measured, a rushed reply that gets the order status wrong, or approves a
return that should've gone to a manager, is what a one-hour panic produces. The team optimizes for the
number on the dashboard, not the customer actually being helped, and a wrong answer sent fast usually costs
more time than a right one sent ten minutes later, since now someone has to catch the mistake and fix it.

## First response time and resolution time are two different promises

Most of the damage from a copied SLA comes from treating "respond" and "resolve" as the same thing. They
aren't, and a small team needs to separate them from day one.

**First response time** is how long until a human (or a system that actually answers, more on that below)
acknowledges the message and, ideally, gives real information, not just "we got your email, someone will be
in touch." A good first response on an order-status question doesn't just confirm receipt, it answers the
question: where the order actually is, pulled from real data, the same problem [order tracking](/blog/wheres-my-order-is-a-supply-chain-question/)
solves when the data lives in one place instead of three systems nobody has open at once.

**Resolution time** is how long until the issue is actually closed: the replacement shipped, the credit
applied, the return approved, the pricing question answered with a number the customer can act on. Some
tickets resolve inside the first response (a stock check, a price confirmation). Others can't, no matter how
fast your team is, because they depend on something outside your control, like a supplier confirming a
[return authorization](/blog/what-a-return-request-actually-needs/) or a carrier locating a lost pallet.

Publishing one blended "we respond within X" number and letting customers assume X also means resolved is
where most of the "you didn't hit your SLA" complaints actually come from. The fix isn't a faster team, it's
two separate, honestly stated numbers.

## Setting SLAs by channel, with real numbers

Email and chat aren't the same promise, and pricing them the same way is where the one-size-fits-all SLA
falls apart fastest.

**Email.** A small distribution team with no dedicated night or weekend coverage should be looking at a
first response of 4 business hours for a routine question and same-business-day (meaning: before the team
logs off, not literally within 60 minutes) for anything that lands after lunch. Resolution time depends on
the ticket: a stock or pricing question should resolve inside that same first response, while something
needing a supplier or carrier answer gets its own honestly-stated window, 1 to 2 business days is a
defensible number to publish rather than promise something you'll break on the first ticket that needs a
carrier callback.

**Chat.** If you run live chat at all, the expectation is inherently faster because the customer is sitting
there watching a typing indicator, not a promise you can walk back the way you can with email. A small team
running chat during business hours only (not 24/7, don't claim hours you don't staff) should target under 5
minutes for first response and be explicit on the widget itself about when chat is staffed, so a message
sent at 9pm gets an honest "we're back at 8am" instead of silence that reads as being ignored.

**Phone and urgent escalations.** These deserve their own tier regardless of channel: a damaged shipment, a
wrong order on a large account, or anything a customer explicitly flags as urgent should jump the queue with
a same-day resolution target, even if routine tickets sit at next-business-day. A small team's SLA structure
should have this escalation lane built in from the start, not bolted on after the first VIP account
complains about waiting behind a routine stock question.

## A worked example: sizing the SLA to the team you actually have

Say you're a 12-person wholesale distributor. Two people split customer service duty alongside other work,
each covering roughly 4 hours a day on the inbox between order processing and other tasks, so you've got
about 8 person-hours of coverage across an 8-hour business day, not 16.

If that team gets 30 tickets a day (a realistic volume for [the same three questions](/blog/same-three-questions-your-team-answers-daily/)
repeating across your account base: where's my order, is this in stock, what's my price), and each one takes
roughly 10 to 15 minutes to actually answer once you count looking something up, that's 5 to 7.5 hours of
raw ticket-answering time against 8 hours of coverage. That's a tight but workable ratio on a normal day.
It's not workable the day one person is out and volume doesn't drop with them, which is exactly when a
1-hour SLA collapses and a 4-hour one still holds.

Run this math for your own team before publishing a number: total daily ticket volume, average handle time
per ticket, and actual person-hours of coverage (not headcount, hours someone is actually free to answer,
which is almost always lower than you'd guess once you account for the rest of their job). If the math
doesn't leave real slack for a bad day, the SLA is aspirational, not operational, and an aspirational SLA is
a promise you're choosing to break in advance.

## Build in the slack, don't round down to zero

The instinct once you've done that math is to promise the best-case number, since that's what the team hits
most days. Resist it. An SLA sized to your best day guarantees you'll miss it on every day that isn't your
best day, which by definition is close to half of them. Size the published number to what the team hits
consistently, including a bad day, and let the team's actual average performance run ahead of the promise
most of the time. Customers notice when you beat your own stated time. Nobody notices, or forgives, when you
promise your best case and deliver your average.

This is also why an SLA needs revisiting on a schedule, not just when it breaks. Ticket volume creeps up as
the account base grows the same way [shipping costs creep up](/blog/shipping-costs-creeping-up/) lane by
lane without anyone noticing the trend, and a 4-hour SLA that was comfortable at 20 tickets a day gets tight
at 40 without anyone deciding to tighten it. Check the real math above quarterly, not just after a customer
complaint forces the conversation.

## Where the math changes: taking the routine tickets off the clock entirely

Everything above assumes a human has to touch every ticket, which is the real constraint behind every number
in this post. Most of what fills a small distributor's queue, order status, stock checks, pricing
confirmations, doesn't need a person's judgment at all, it needs a fast, accurate lookup against data the
business already has. [Customer Service](/customer-service/) answers those directly from live order,
shipment, and inventory data the moment the question comes in, at whatever hour it arrives, and routes only
the tickets that genuinely need a judgment call, an angry account, an exception to a standing agreement, a
return that doesn't fit the normal pattern, to your team.

That changes what an SLA can honestly promise. A first-response SLA on a routine lookup stops being "how
fast can a person get to it" and becomes closer to immediate, because there's no queue for that category of
question anymore. The human coverage hours you calculated above then apply only to the tickets that actually
need a person, which is a much smaller number than your total volume, and a much easier one to build a
promise around that survives someone calling in sick.

## FAQ

**What's a realistic first-response SLA for a small business?**
For email, 4 business hours for routine questions is realistic for a small team without dedicated coverage,
tightened to under 5 minutes for live chat during staffed hours only. The right number depends on your
actual ticket volume and coverage hours, not a copied industry benchmark, and [Customer Service](/customer-service/)
changes the math by answering routine lookups immediately rather than queuing them for a person.

**What's the difference between first response time and resolution time?**
First response time is how long until someone acknowledges and ideally answers the question. Resolution
time is how long until the issue is fully closed, a shipped replacement, an applied credit, an approved
return. A stock or pricing question can resolve inside the first response; something depending on a
[return authorization](/blog/what-a-return-request-actually-needs/) or a carrier answer needs its own,
separately stated window.

**Should chat and email have the same SLA?**
No. Chat is a real-time channel a customer is actively watching, so it needs a much tighter first-response
target, minutes rather than hours, and only during hours you actually staff it. Email can honestly run on a
same-business-day or next-business-day cadence without feeling broken, as long as that's the number you
publish rather than an email SLA copied from a chat benchmark.

**Why does copying a big company's SLA hurt more than not having one?**
A posted promise your team can't consistently hit reads as a broken commitment every time it slips, which
damages trust more than never stating a number at all. It also pressures the team to answer fast instead of
right, which is how [order tracking](/blog/wheres-my-order-is-a-supply-chain-question/) mistakes and
misrouted returns happen under a one-hour panic.

**How often should an SLA be reviewed?**
Quarterly at minimum, and any time ticket volume shifts noticeably, since a comfortable response window at
today's volume can get tight within a few months as the account base grows without anyone deciding to
re-run the coverage math.
