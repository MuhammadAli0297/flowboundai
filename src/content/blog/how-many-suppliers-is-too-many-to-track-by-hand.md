Ask a small operator how many suppliers they manage, and most know the number off the top of their head.
Ask how confident they are that every one of those relationships is on track right now, lead times current,
nothing overdue, and the confidence usually drops. Somewhere between "a few suppliers" and "a few dozen,"
tracking it all by memory and spreadsheet quietly stops being reliable, even though nothing about the process
changed.

## The number that feels fine until it isn't

There's no clean threshold, five suppliers or fifteen, where things suddenly break. It's more like odds
catching up with you. Each supplier relationship has its own small chance of a lead time drifting or an
order slipping through the cracks in any given month. With three suppliers, you'll probably catch it. With
twenty, something is quietly wrong on any given week, you just don't know which one.

## What actually breaks first

It's rarely the big, obvious supplier that causes a problem. It's the smaller one you check on less often,
whose lead time changed eight months ago and never got updated in your notes, because nothing about that
relationship demanded regular attention until the moment it did.

## A rough way to tell you're past it

If you can't say, right now, which of your suppliers has the longest lead time or which one's had the most
delays this quarter, without opening several tabs and doing some mental math, you're past the point where
memory and spreadsheets are actually managing the relationships. They're just recording what already
happened.

## Watching every supplier the same way, at any count

The fix isn't managing fewer suppliers. It's not relying on memory and manual review to catch the one that
needs attention. [Supplier Coordination](/supplier-coordination/) keeps every supplier conversation, purchase
order, and lead time change in one place and flags the ones that need you, so the count of suppliers you're
managing stops being the thing that determines whether something gets missed.
