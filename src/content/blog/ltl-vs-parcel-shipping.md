Ship parcel under about 150 pounds and LTL freight above it, but that single number is the start of the
decision, not the end of it. The real breakpoint moves with your product's freight class, how many boxes
are going to the same address, and whether the destination needs a liftgate or residential delivery. A
90-pound pallet of a bulky, low-density item can already be cheaper on LTL, while a 180-pound order split
across two easy-to-handle boxes can still beat freight on parcel. Getting this decision right on a
recurring basis is worth real money: it's routinely a 30 to 65 percent cost swing on the same shipment,
depending on which mode you pick.

## The weight and box-count breakpoint, with real numbers

Parcel carriers cap a single package at 150 pounds and 108 inches in length, with length plus girth (twice
the width plus twice the height) capped around 165 inches combined. Past either limit, the carrier either
refuses the package or reclassifies it as freight automatically, usually at a much worse rate than if you'd
booked LTL from the start.

Below that ceiling, parcel carriers don't just bill actual weight. They bill the greater of actual weight or
dimensional weight, calculated as length times width times height in inches, divided by a carrier-set divisor
(139 is standard for most domestic services). A box measuring 24 by 18 by 18 inches works out to 7,776 cubic
inches, divided by 139 is just under 56 pounds of dimensional weight. If the actual contents only weigh 30
pounds, you're still billed for 56, because the box takes up truck space a 56-pound shipment would. This is
the part that trips up light, bulky products specifically: foam packaging, plastic housewares, textiles.
They can hit LTL-territory pricing on parcel well before they hit 150 actual pounds, just from the box size
alone.

Box count matters as much as weight for a different reason. A single 40-pound box ships parcel without a
second thought. Five of those boxes going to the same wholesale account on the same day add up to 200
pounds and, more importantly, five separate parcel rates stacked on top of each other, no volume discount
for shipping them together. Consolidate the same order onto one pallet and it becomes a single LTL shipment
priced by total weight and class, not five individual parcel line items. That gap is usually where the
biggest, easiest-to-miss savings sit: not a single heavy box, but a habit of shipping multi-box orders as
separate parcels because that's how the account has always been packed.

## Freight class and NMFC codes: why two shipments of the same weight can cost differently

Once a shipment moves to LTL, weight alone doesn't set the price. Freight class does, and freight class is
mostly a function of density, not what the product is. Density is calculated as total weight divided by
cubic feet, where cubic feet is length times width times height in inches, divided by 1,728.

Take two pallets, both weighing 400 pounds, both measuring 40 by 48 by 50 inches. That's 55.6 cubic feet
either way. But if one pallet holds dense canned goods and the other holds low-density packaging or
lightweight plastic parts, they land in different freight classes even at identical weight and footprint,
because carriers price freight partly on how much trailer space it consumes relative to its weight, not
just the weight itself. A denser shipment (higher pounds per cubic foot) gets a lower class number and a
cheaper rate; a low-density shipment gets a higher class number and pays noticeably more per pound to move
the same distance. On an 18-class scale running from Class 50 to Class 500, the gap between a low class and
a high class on an identical lane can be several times the base rate, not a rounding difference.

Every shippable item also carries an NMFC (National Motor Freight Classification) code, the specific
commodity identifier a carrier uses to confirm the class you're claiming actually matches what's on the
pallet. As of July 2025, the NMFTA shifted the classification system to weigh density more heavily across
most commodity categories, so a product's actual measured density now drives its class more directly than
it used to, and a class that was accurate two years ago on the same product may not be anymore. Get the
class wrong, intentionally or not, and the carrier reweighs and reclasses the shipment after pickup,
usually at a rate correction that lands on your invoice as a surprise days after the shipment already left.
That's a big part of why freight quoting looks unpredictable to teams used to parcel's flat, published rate
tables. It isn't unpredictable, it's just priced on inputs (density, class, accessorials) that parcel
pricing doesn't have.

## The mistake that costs the most: shipping heavy freight through parcel out of habit

The most common version of this mistake isn't a single oversized box, it's a wholesale account that's
always been shipped as multiple parcels because that's how the very first order went out, years ago, before
volume grew. A distributor shipping a routine 6-box, 210-pound restock order to the same commercial kitchen
supply account via parcel might be paying $190 to $240 across six separate parcel invoices. Palletize the
same order and book it LTL, and the same freight often lands at $110 to $140 delivered, even after adding a
liftgate fee for a dock-less receiving address. Nobody re-checks this because the order ships fine every
time. It's not a failure that shows up as a complaint, it's just a quiet, recurring margin leak on an
account that looks completely normal from the outside. This is the same blind spot behind [creeping
shipping costs](/blog/shipping-costs-creeping-up/) more broadly: the cost isn't wrong on any single
shipment, it's wrong as a pattern nobody's re-checking.

## The other mistake: LTL-ing something that should have stayed parcel

The reverse mistake shows up less often but hurts just as much per shipment. A single lightweight box gets
put on a pallet and booked as LTL because "it's going to the same account as the freight order," triggering
an LTL minimum charge, plus a residential or liftgate accessorial if the destination needs one, easily $150
to $250 for a shipment a parcel carrier would have moved for $35 to $50. LTL accessorials are exactly where
this goes wrong: a residential delivery surcharge, an inside-delivery fee, or a liftgate requirement can add
$50 to $200 on top of the base freight rate, and those fees don't shrink for a small, light shipment the way
the base rate does. A wholesale account with no dock and no forklift, the kind [Wholesale Account
Management](/wholesale-account-management/) tracks the standing terms for, is exactly the profile where a
well-intentioned "just put it with the freight order" decision backfires, because every accessorial that
order needs applies in full regardless of how little is actually on the pallet.

## Why this has to be a per-order decision, not a fixed rule

A flat rule like "always LTL over 150 pounds" is easier to write into a picking process than "check every
order," which is exactly why it's so common and exactly why it keeps leaking money in both directions. The
real inputs, box count to a single address, the specific SKU's density and resulting freight class, and
whether that specific delivery address needs an accessorial, change order to order, even for the same
customer. The same wholesale account might get a 3-box order this week that's clearly parcel and an 8-box
restock next month that's clearly LTL, and a fixed threshold set for one of those will get the other one
wrong. This is the same trap [carrier selection](/blog/cost-of-picking-the-wrong-carrier/) runs into when a
business locks in one carrier relationship and stops re-checking it: a rule that was right once doesn't
stay right as order patterns shift, it just stops getting questioned.

Treating this as a per-order check doesn't require a logistics team or a transportation management system.
It requires knowing, for the specific order in front of you, the total weight and box count to that
address, the freight class your product actually carries at its real packed density, and whether the
receiving address has a dock or needs an accessorial, then comparing an actual parcel quote against an
actual LTL quote before defaulting to whichever mode "usually" gets used. A shipment that's cutting it close
on timing shouldn't skip this check either. Paying for [rush shipping](/blog/rush-shipping-is-a-symptom-not-a-solution/)
on the wrong mode compounds both problems into one invoice instead of fixing either.

## FAQ

**What is the weight limit for parcel shipping before I need LTL freight?**
Most parcel carriers cap a single package at 150 pounds and around 165 inches of combined length and girth.
Below that, dimensional weight pricing (based on box size, not just actual weight) can still push a light,
bulky item into LTL-competitive pricing well under 150 pounds, which is one reason a fixed weight rule alone
isn't reliable, see [Shipping Optimization](/shipping-optimization/) for how this gets checked per order.

**How does freight class affect what I pay for LTL shipping?**
Freight class runs from Class 50 to Class 500 and is driven mainly by density, weight divided by cubic
feet. A low-density shipment gets a higher class and a noticeably higher rate per pound than a dense
shipment of the same weight and footprint, which is why two pallets that look similar can price very
differently, a detail that gets missed in general [carrier selection](/blog/cost-of-picking-the-wrong-carrier/)
advice that assumes flat, weight-only pricing.

**Is it cheaper to ship multiple boxes as one LTL pallet instead of separate parcels?**
Often yes, once you're sending three or more boxes to the same address on the same day. Separate parcels
each carry their own base rate with no volume benefit, while consolidating onto one pallet prices the whole
shipment together under LTL, frequently 30 to 65 percent cheaper than the parcel total for the same freight.

**What accessorial fees should I check before booking LTL freight?**
Liftgate service, residential delivery, and inside delivery are the three that most often turn an
LTL shipment expensive, each commonly adding $50 to $200 on top of the base rate. Check the receiving
address's actual setup, not just its ZIP code, since these fees apply in full even on a small shipment,
the same standing-terms detail [Wholesale Account Management](/wholesale-account-management/) keeps current
per account.

**Should shipping mode be a fixed company policy or decided per order?**
Per order. Box count, product density, and destination accessorials all shift the real breakeven point
order to order, even for the same customer, so a flat "always parcel under X pounds" rule will eventually
get both directions wrong, the exact pattern behind [creeping shipping costs](/blog/shipping-costs-creeping-up/)
that nobody notices until someone finally re-checks the numbers.
