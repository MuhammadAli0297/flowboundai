Most small distributors can tell you what their inventory software costs. Almost none of them can tell you
what running the operation on spreadsheets, phone calls, and someone's memory actually costs, because that
number never shows up on an invoice. It shows up as hours nobody bills separately, a stockout nobody
connects to the cause, and a double order nobody notices until the cash is already spent. The real cost of
manual supply chain management for a typical 10 to 20 person wholesale or distribution team runs somewhere
between $20,000 and $60,000 a year once you add up the labor hours and the errors those hours don't catch,
and most operators are only aware of a fraction of it.

## What "manual" actually costs in hours

Start with the part that's at least countable: the hours. At a small distributor, the manual version of
supply chain management usually breaks into four recurring tasks, done by whoever has time that day rather
than one dedicated role:

- Checking stock levels against a spreadsheet or a shared drive to see what's getting low
- Calling or emailing suppliers to place orders and confirm lead times
- Updating the tracking sheet when a shipment comes in, a price changes, or an order ships
- Answering "did we already order this" and "where's my order" questions that a live system would answer on its own

Take Riverside Outdoor Supply, a 14-person wholesale distributor of camping gear, as a real example. Two
people split these tasks: an operations lead who spends about 6 hours a week on reordering and supplier
calls, and a warehouse coordinator who spends another 5 hours a week updating the tracking sheet and
answering "where's my order" questions from customers and reps. That's 11 hours a week, 572 hours a year,
before anyone accounts for the time lost when the sheet is wrong and someone has to recheck it by hand. This
is the same territory [the gap between SMB needs and enterprise supply chain advice](/blog/smb-supply-chain-gap/)
covers from a different angle: the tasks don't disappear at a small company, they just get absorbed into
whoever's available instead of owned by a system.

## The direct labor math

The formula is simple on purpose, because the point is that you can run it yourself in five minutes:

**Weekly hours on manual tracking x blended hourly cost x 52 = annual labor cost of the manual process**

At Riverside, 11 hours a week at a blended fully-loaded rate of $24 an hour (wages plus payroll tax and
benefits, not just the paycheck number) comes to $264 a week, or $13,728 a year. That's the visible part,
the hours you could point to on a timesheet if anyone tracked time that granularly, which most small teams
don't. It's also usually the smaller half of the real number.

## The costs that don't show up on a timesheet

The bigger cost of manual tracking isn't the hours spent doing it right. It's what happens the weeks it goes
wrong, and on a spreadsheet nobody's watching continuously, it goes wrong more often than most owners assume.

**Missed reorders and stockouts.** A reorder point tracked on a static spreadsheet only reflects reality the
moment someone last updated it. Between updates, a bestseller can sell faster than usual and blow past the
point where a new order should have gone out days ago, especially if the [reorder point](/blog/reorder-point-math/)
was set once months ago and never revisited as sales pace shifted. A single stockout on a $40 wholesale unit
selling 15 units a week costs roughly $600 a week in lost revenue while it lasts, plus whatever rush shipping
or customer goodwill it takes to make it right once the order finally lands. [Reorder](/reorder/) and
[Demand Forecasting](/demand-forecasting/) exist specifically to close that gap, watching the number
continuously instead of whenever someone remembers to look.

**Double ordering.** The mirror image of a missed reorder: two people work off the same spreadsheet, one
places an order Tuesday morning, the other doesn't see the update and places a second order Tuesday
afternoon. Now there's twice the inventory sitting in a warehouse that only needed one order's worth, cash
tied up that could have covered a different SKU's reorder instead. At even a modest $2,500 average order
size, two or three of these a year quietly ties up $5,000 to $7,500 in cash that never needed to move.
[Inventory Tracking](/inventory-tracking/) is the fix here, one live number both people are actually looking
at instead of two copies of a file drifting apart.

**Pricing errors.** When supplier costs move and nobody catches it in the sheet for a few weeks, the sell
price stays put while the margin underneath it quietly erodes. A 3 percent cost increase that goes
unnoticed for a month on $80,000 of monthly wholesale volume is roughly $2,400 in margin gone before anyone
adjusts anything, an outcome [manually repricing every SKU](/blog/cost-of-repricing-manually/) makes almost
guaranteed once the catalog gets past a couple hundred items, because nobody can re-run that math by hand
every time a cost sheet changes.

**Supplier coordination that falls through the cracks.** Once a business is tracking more than a handful of
supplier relationships by email and phone, a lead-time change, a partial shipment, or a price update sent by
one supplier and never logged anywhere central becomes normal, not rare. [Supplier Coordination](/supplier-coordination/)
turns that into one place every conversation and change lives, which matters most exactly when a team has
outgrown the point where one person can hold every supplier relationship in their head.

**Staff turnover risk.** This one is real but rarely counted. Manually reconciling a spreadsheet, chasing
suppliers for status updates, and fielding the same "where's my order" question all day is genuinely
tedious work, and it's disproportionately the kind of role that burns people out and turns over. Replacing
an operations coordinator, between the open req, the interviews, and the months it takes a new hire to
actually run at full speed, easily costs several months of that role's salary in lost productivity alone,
on top of the actual hiring cost. A team that keeps losing the person in this seat isn't unlucky. It's
usually a sign the job itself is more manual than it should be.

## A framework for estimating your own team's real cost

You don't need Riverside's exact numbers, you need your own. Four questions, in order, get you there:

1. **How many hours a week, across everyone touching it, go into checking stock, placing orders, and
   updating the tracking sheet or file?** Ask each person directly rather than guessing; most owners
   underestimate this by half because the work is scattered across a day instead of one visible block.
2. **What's the blended fully-loaded hourly cost of those people?** Wages plus payroll tax and benefits,
   not just the paycheck number.
3. **How many stockouts, double orders, or pricing misses happened in the last 90 days, and what did each
   one actually cost?** Even a rough count beats no count. Multiply the quarterly total by four for an
   annual estimate.
4. **Add step 1 x step 2 x 52 to step 3's annual estimate.** That total is your real number, not the
   software line item, the actual cost of running the process the way it runs today.

Most teams that run this exercise land somewhere in the $20,000 to $60,000 range for a business this size,
and the second half of that number, the error costs, is usually the part nobody had priced out before.

## What actually changes when the manual work goes away

The honest caveat here: not every piece of software marketed as a fix for this actually removes the manual
work, some of it just moves the same spreadsheet into a nicer-looking interface and calls it done, a
pattern worth knowing [the difference between real software and a spreadsheet with extra steps](/blog/supply-chain-software-spreadsheet-with-extra-steps/)
before buying anything. The hours only actually disappear when the reorder point, the stock count, the
supplier status, and the sell price are each being watched and acted on continuously by something other
than a person's memory, which is the specific gap Flowbound is built to close rather than another dashboard
to check before making the same decisions by hand.

## FAQ

**How much does manual supply chain management really cost a small distributor?**
Typically $20,000 to $60,000 a year for a 10 to 20 person team, once you add the direct labor hours spent
on tracking and reordering to the cost of the errors those hours miss: stockouts, double orders, and
pricing drift. Wholesale accounts specifically add another layer, since a missed pricing tier or order
minimum on a [wholesale account](/wholesale-account-management/) is its own quiet cost most teams never
trace back to the spreadsheet that caused it.

**What's the biggest hidden cost of tracking inventory on a spreadsheet?**
Usually stockouts and double orders, not the hours themselves. A spreadsheet only reflects reality the
moment someone last updated it, so the gap between updates is where a bestseller sells out unnoticed or two
people place the same order without knowing it, the same gap that makes [reviewing every purchase order by
hand stop scaling](/blog/why-approving-every-purchase-order-doesnt-scale/) once order volume grows past what
one person can watch.

**How many hours a week does manual reordering actually take?**
For a small distribution team, commonly 10 to 15 hours a week combined across everyone touching it: checking
stock, placing and confirming orders, and updating a tracking sheet. Most owners underestimate this because
it's spread across a day in small chunks rather than sitting in one visible block of time.

**Does switching to software automatically remove these costs?**
Only if the software actually watches the numbers continuously and acts on them, not if it's a nicer
interface wrapped around the same manual updates. The same discipline applies to shipping decisions: comparing
carriers and lanes by hand costs time the same way manual reordering does, which is what
[Shipping Optimization](/shipping-optimization/) is built to remove instead of just display more clearly.

**Is this cost worth calculating if the team feels like it's managing fine?**
Yes, precisely because "managing fine" is usually true until a bestseller stockout or a missed price update
makes it visible all at once. Running the four-step framework above takes less than an hour and turns a
vague feeling into a real number worth comparing against the cost of fixing it.
