Most distributors renegotiate wholesale terms exactly once: when the relationship is already strained, a
price hike lands out of nowhere, and someone finally picks up the phone. That's the worst time to do it.
The right time is when you have leverage and haven't spent it yet: real volume growth, a documented
competitor offer, or a clean payment history you can point to, backed by your own order numbers instead of
a general complaint that prices feel high.

## When you actually have a case to renegotiate

There are three situations worth a renegotiation conversation, and each one needs its own kind of proof:

**Volume growth.** If your trailing six to twelve months of orders from a supplier are running meaningfully
above what they were when your current pricing tier was set, usually 25% or more, you're paying yesterday's
rate for today's business. A supplier who quoted you a price break at 500 units a month has no reason to
extend it to 800 units a month unless you show them the 800.

**A competitor's better offer.** A written quote from another supplier for a comparable SKU, at a comparable
volume, is the single strongest thing you can bring to the table. Not a rumor about what a competitor pays,
an actual quote with a number on it.

**A clean payment history.** Twelve or more months of on-time payments, no disputes, no rush orders that cost
the supplier extra to expedite, is worth real money to a supplier's own cash flow planning. It's weaker
leverage than the first two on its own, but it's the thing that makes a supplier believe you'll actually
follow through on a bigger commitment, which strengthens whichever of the other two you're leading with.

Any one of these can justify a conversation. Two together make it a strong one. None of them justifies
asking every quarter: a supplier who gets renegotiation requests every ninety days starts treating your
account as a negotiation to be managed rather than a relationship to invest in, and that costs you more
than the pricing you're chasing.

## Build the case with your own numbers, not a feeling

"Prices feel high" gets a supplier's guard up. A specific, dated set of numbers gets a real conversation.
Before you ask for anything, pull your own order history for that supplier and put it in a form you can
actually hand over or read from on a call:

- Total units and total spend for the last 12 months, broken out by quarter so growth is visible, not just
  a single lump total.
- The percentage growth from the quarter your current pricing was negotiated to the most recent quarter.
- Your on-time payment rate over the same window, if it's genuinely strong. If it isn't, don't cite it, a
  supplier will remember the two late payments faster than you'd like.
- The specific ask, expressed as a number, not a feeling: "move us from the 500-unit tier to the 1,000-unit
  tier" or "extend net-30 to net-45" or "drop the 200-unit order minimum to 100 now that we're ordering
  weekly instead of monthly."

A worked example: a hardware distributor ordering 480 units a month from a fastener supplier at $0.62 a
unit, on a tier that kicks in at 500 units, has been sitting one order shy of the next price break for four
straight months. That's not a growth story, it's a rounding error away from a tier they already qualify for
in spirit. Compare that to a distributor who started at 300 units a month eight months ago and is now
averaging 780: that's a 160% increase, a real conversation, and a supplier who already has the invoices to
confirm it themselves. The first case is a five-minute email. The second is worth a scheduled call, because
it's asking the supplier to reprice a meaningfully bigger piece of their own business, not just round up.

This is also where having actual order history on hand, rather than reconstructing it from memory or a pile
of invoices right before the call, matters. A [wholesale account](/blog/what-a-wholesale-account-actually-needs/)
that's tracked consistently means you're not scrambling to rebuild six months of order data the night before
you ask; you're just pulling the number that was already there.

## Timing the ask

Bring it up near a natural checkpoint, not in the middle of an unrelated order. The end of a contract term,
a supplier's own pricing review cycle if you know when that happens, or right after a quarter that clearly
shows the growth you're pointing to are all better moments than an ordinary Tuesday. If the supplier just
had a rough quarter of their own, a rate hike on their end, a [lead time that's been slipping](/blog/supplier-lead-time-slips/),
or a shortage they're managing, that's not the week to ask for a break: not because you don't deserve one,
but because a supplier under pressure has less room to say yes, and a "no" now makes the next ask harder
than if you'd waited a month.

Talk to the person who actually owns pricing, not just your regular order contact. A day-to-day rep can
often escalate the request for you, but they usually can't approve a tier change or a minimum reduction
themselves, and routing the ask through the wrong person just adds a round trip before the real conversation
even starts.

## Common mistakes that cost you the leverage you had

**Asking before the growth is real.** One good month isn't a trend. Asking off a single strong quarter,
especially right after a slow stretch, reads as opportunistic rather than earned, and a supplier who says no
to a premature ask is less likely to say yes to the same ask six months later when it's actually justified,
because you've already used the conversation.

**Showing up with no data.** "We've been ordering a lot more lately" invites a supplier to lowball whatever
they think "a lot more" means, and they will, because they have no incentive to guess generously. Bring the
actual numbers described above every time, even for a request that feels obvious.

**Threatening to leave without meaning it.** Naming a competitor's quote is legitimate leverage. Implying
you'll walk if you have no real intention or ability to switch suppliers is a bluff, and an experienced
supplier can usually tell the difference, especially with a SKU that has real switching costs (tooling,
minimum order commitments elsewhere, a qualification process for a new vendor). A bluff that gets called
costs you more credibility than never making the threat at all, and it tends to get remembered the next time
you ask for anything, not just this one.

**Asking for everything at once.** Bundling a price break, a longer payment term, and a lower order minimum
into one conversation gives the supplier three things to say no to instead of one thing to say yes to. Lead
with whichever one your data supports most clearly, get that agreed, and treat the next ask as its own
conversation later.

## Tracking terms once you've won them

A renegotiated tier or minimum is only worth what it's worth if every order after that conversation actually
gets billed at the new terms. That's a surprisingly common failure point: the new pricing gets agreed
verbally or over email, and three months later an invoice comes in at the old rate because whoever processes
purchase orders on the supplier's side never got the update, or because your own team is still ordering in
the old case-pack size that no longer matches the new minimum. Coordinating that handoff cleanly, and
keeping every [supplier conversation and agreement in one place](/supplier-coordination/) instead of an email
thread that gets buried, is what actually makes a renegotiated term stick instead of quietly reverting.

This is also where a clear view into demand matters for making the next ask, not just proving the last one:
knowing your real growth trajectory (the same forward-looking number [demand forecasting](/demand-forecasting/)
already tracks) is what tells you when the next tier conversation is actually due, rather than guessing at
it a year later.

[Wholesale Account Management](/wholesale-account-management/) exists for exactly this part: it tracks
pricing tiers, order minimums, and standing agreements per account, so once a new term is agreed, every
subsequent order goes out at that rate automatically instead of depending on someone remembering to update
a spreadsheet or flag the change to whoever cuts the purchase order. It doesn't negotiate the terms for you,
that's still a conversation between you and the supplier, but it makes sure the terms you actually won don't
quietly drift back to the old number three invoices later.

## FAQ

**How much volume growth justifies renegotiating wholesale pricing?**
A sustained increase of 25% or more over six to twelve months, compared to the volume your current pricing
tier was set against, is generally strong enough to bring to a supplier. A single strong month isn't, and
asking too early on a trend that hasn't held tends to cost more credibility than waiting, since the same
supplier remembers a premature ask when you come back with real numbers later. Keeping that comparison
current is part of what a properly tracked [wholesale account](/wholesale-account-management/) is for.

**Should I mention a competitor's price when negotiating with a supplier?**
Yes, if it's a real, written quote for a comparable SKU at a comparable volume. A vague reference to what
"other distributors pay" carries no weight and can read as a bluff. If you don't actually have a competing
quote in hand, don't imply you do, an experienced supplier can usually tell the difference and it costs you
credibility on the next ask too.

**Can I renegotiate order minimums instead of price?**
Yes, and it's often an easier ask than a price change, since it doesn't touch the supplier's own margin the
way a per-unit discount does. If your order frequency has increased, for example moving from monthly to
weekly orders, a lower per-order minimum can matter more to your cash flow than a small price break, and
it's worth asking for on its own rather than only ever pushing for a lower unit cost. The [reorder point
math](/blog/reorder-point-math/) behind how often you're actually ordering is a good starting point for
making that case.

**How often is it reasonable to ask a supplier to renegotiate terms?**
Once every 12 months for a routine growth-based ask is reasonable for most supplier relationships. A
significant, well-documented event, a competitor's quote or a major volume jump, can justify asking sooner,
but repeated asks close together train a supplier to treat your account as a negotiation to manage rather
than a relationship to invest in.

**What happens if the supplier says no?**
Ask what would change their answer, a higher volume threshold, a longer commitment, a different payment
term, rather than dropping the conversation entirely. That answer tells you exactly what to track and come
back with next time, and keeping that follow-up organized alongside every other open item is exactly what
[supplier coordination](/supplier-coordination/) is for, so the next attempt isn't starting from scratch.
