An order is about to run out, a customer is waiting, and rush shipping feels like the responsible move: pay
more, get it there faster, problem solved. It does solve the immediate problem. It doesn't touch the actual
one, which is usually that a reorder should have gone out several days earlier and didn't.

## The expensive fix that treats the wrong problem

Rush shipping treats the symptom, a shipment that's cutting it close, without asking why it's cutting it
close in the first place. Do this often enough and it stops looking like an occasional emergency and starts
looking like a recurring cost, one that a slightly earlier reorder would have avoided entirely.

## Where the real delay actually started

Almost every rush shipment traces back to the same root cause: a reorder that went out later than it should
have, usually because a reorder point was stale, a lead time had drifted without anyone updating it, or
nobody was watching the stock level closely enough to catch it in time. By the time rush shipping enters the
picture, the real mistake already happened days earlier.

## What rush shipping costs beyond the shipping bill

The shipping premium is the visible cost. The less visible one is that it trains the business to treat a
planning failure as a shipping decision, which means the actual root cause, a stale reorder point or a
drifting lead time, never gets fixed and shows up again next month.

## Catching the reorder before it needs rescuing

The real fix isn't a faster shipping option. It's not needing rush shipping in the first place, because the
reorder went out when it was supposed to. [Reorder](/reorder/) places the purchase order the moment your
reorder point is actually hit, so the shipment that would have needed rescuing goes out on a normal timeline
instead.
