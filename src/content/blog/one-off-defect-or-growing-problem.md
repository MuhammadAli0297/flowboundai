A damaged shipment shows up, someone processes the return, and life moves on. That's the right response to
a one-off. The trouble is that most small teams handle the tenth damaged shipment from the same supplier the
same way they handled the first: as an isolated incident, because nothing in how it was recorded connects it
to the nine before it.

## Every defect looks like an isolated incident, at first

Returns and inspection failures get logged, if they get logged at all, in whatever system is closest at
hand: a spreadsheet, a note in the order record, a comment on an invoice. Each entry makes sense on its own.
None of them are set up to be compared against each other, so a pattern has to be noticed by memory, by
someone happening to recall that this feels familiar.

## The math that turns an incident into a pattern

A single defect rate of two percent from a supplier might be completely normal. The same two percent, if it
used to be half a percent three months ago, is a trend, and trends are what turn into a real problem if
nobody's tracking the baseline to notice the drift. The individual incident was never the risk. The
unnoticed trend underneath it was.

## Where the pattern hides in a small operation

Small teams are actually well positioned to catch this, if the data is in one place: fewer suppliers, fewer
SKUs, and someone who already knows the business well enough to spot something off, if they're shown the
numbers instead of having to remember them across months of individual tickets.
