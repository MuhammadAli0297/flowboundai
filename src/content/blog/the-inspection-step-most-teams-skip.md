Incoming inspection, actually checking a shipment against what was ordered before it goes into stock, is one
of the first things to slip when a team gets busy. It feels safe to skip: the supplier has usually been
reliable, the shipment looks fine at a glance, and there's always something more urgent to get to. Most of
the time, skipping it costs nothing. The times it doesn't are expensive enough to matter.

## The check that's easiest to skip

Nobody decides to stop inspecting shipments. It happens gradually, a busy week where the shipment goes
straight onto the shelf, then another, until checking incoming stock against the purchase order becomes the
exception instead of the rule. Nothing about this is careless. It's just what happens when a step has no
immediate consequence for being skipped, until it does.

## What it actually catches, and when

Inspection catches the problem at its cheapest possible point: before the item has been shelved, sold, or
shipped to a customer. A defect caught at inspection costs a conversation with the supplier. The same defect
caught after a customer receives it costs a return, a replacement, and a customer who's now less confident
in the order.

## The cost difference between catching it early and catching it late

The math isn't subtle. An inspection catch is a line item. A customer-discovered defect is a return, a
replacement shipment, the support time to handle both, and whatever it does to the customer's trust in
future orders. The inspection step that felt optional is the cheapest point in the entire chain to catch a
problem.

## Logging every inspection without adding a step

The goal isn't more manual checking, it's making sure the checking that happens actually gets recorded and
compared over time. [Quality Monitoring](/quality-monitoring/) logs incoming inspection results automatically
and flags anything that fails before it ever reaches your shelf, so the step that's easiest to skip doesn't
have to depend on nobody being busy that week.
