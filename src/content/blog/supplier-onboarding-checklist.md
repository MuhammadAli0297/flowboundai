A new supplier feels like the easy part of the relationship. Pricing looks good, samples looked fine, and
there's a real temptation to just place the order and see what happens. That instinct is how a business ends
up locked into a vendor whose real minimum order quantity, real lead time, and real quality standard don't
show up until three months in, usually right after a customer complaint.

## What a supplier onboarding checklist actually needs to cover

A supplier onboarding checklist is the set of terms, commitments, and contacts you confirm in writing before
the first purchase order goes out, not after. At minimum that's six things: lead time (stated as a range,
not a single number), minimum order quantity and case pack size, quality and inspection expectations, payment
terms, a named backup contact, and the supplier's policy on sample or trial orders. Get all six in writing
before money moves, and most of the surprises that sink a new vendor relationship never get the chance to
happen.

## Lead time: get the range, not the average

Ask a supplier "what's your lead time" and almost every one of them answers with a single number, usually
the best case. "Two weeks" is what you'll hear from a supplier who ships in nine days when everything goes
right and in twenty-six when it doesn't, and you won't find out which one you actually got until your first
order is already late. Push for a range instead: "what's your fastest turnaround and what's the longest
you've run in the last six months, for a reason that wasn't a holiday or a factory shutdown." A supplier who
can answer that specifically, with real numbers, is telling you something useful. A supplier who can only
repeat the same round number they opened with is telling you they either don't track it or don't want to.
Once you have real orders on the books, this is the same range you'll eventually need to [set an honest
reorder point](/blog/reorder-point-math/): a reorder point built on a vendor's optimistic quote instead of
their actual worst case is a stockout waiting for a bad month.

## Minimum order quantity and case pack size, confirmed before you plan cash flow

MOQ shows up in two places: a supplier-wide minimum ("we don't ship less than 500 units on any SKU") and a
per-SKU case pack ("this one only comes in cartons of 24"). Both need to be confirmed before you build a
purchase order around them, not discovered when the order bounces back from the supplier's side with a
note to reorder at the right multiple. This matters more than it sounds like for a business running lean on
cash: a supplier whose real MOQ is 1,000 units on a SKU you wanted to test at 200 either changes your trial
plan entirely or rules that supplier out for that item. Ask for MOQ and case pack size for the specific SKUs
you're actually planning to order, not the supplier's general policy, since plenty of vendors quote a
low headline minimum that only applies to their bestselling items.

## Quality and inspection expectations, agreed on paper before the first shipment

"Good quality" means nothing until both sides agree on what gets checked and what fails. Before the first
order, get specific on three things: what an incoming inspection will look for (visual defects, packaging
damage, a functional test, a sample count versus a full check), what the acceptable defect rate is (2% is
a common threshold for a first order with an unproven vendor, tighter for anything safety- or
regulation-sensitive), and what happens when a shipment fails: replacement, credit, or return at the
supplier's cost. A supplier who's uncomfortable putting any of this in writing before you've placed a real
order is telling you something about how they'll handle a real quality dispute later. This is worth doing
even for a supplier who
[already has a track record elsewhere](/blog/suppliers-already-have-a-quality-track-record/): a good record
with someone else's spec doesn't guarantee they'll hit yours, and [the inspection step most teams
skip](/blog/the-inspection-step-most-teams-skip/) on a first order is exactly the one a brand-new vendor
relationship can least afford to skip.

## Payment terms, and what happens if the first order goes wrong

Net 30 sounds standard until you actually read what a new supplier is offering, which for an unproven
relationship is often 50% deposit with the balance due before shipment, not net anything. That's a normal
starting position for a supplier who doesn't know you yet, the same way you shouldn't extend generous terms
to a buyer with no history either. What matters is agreeing on the number before the invoice arrives, and
asking one more question most first-time buyers skip: what's the process if the order needs to be disputed
or the goods arrive short or damaged. A supplier who has a clear answer, a defined credit or refund path, is
a different risk profile than one who says "that's never happened" and leaves it there.

## A named backup contact, not just a sales rep's email

Every new supplier relationship starts with one point of contact, usually a sales rep who's motivated to
close the deal. That's fine for negotiating price, but it's not enough for the relationship going forward.
Before the first order ships, ask for a second contact: someone in operations or customer service who can
answer a status question if the sales rep is unreachable, out, or moves to a different account. A supplier
who can't produce a second name is often a one- or two-person operation, which isn't automatically
disqualifying for a small vendor, but it is a fact you should know going in rather than discovering the day
your rep stops responding and there's nobody else to call. This becomes the exact problem
[coordinating more than a couple of supplier relationships](/supplier-coordination/) by hand runs into:
a missing backup contact is invisible until the one person you have goes quiet.

## Structuring the first order as a real trial, not a leap of faith

The first purchase order with a new supplier should be sized to test the relationship, not to fill a season
of demand. A reasonable trial order is small enough that a bad outcome doesn't hurt (a partial container, a
few case packs, whatever the MOQ allows at its lowest tier) but large enough to actually exercise the things
you're checking: does the packaging survive transit, does the lead time land inside the range they quoted,
does the defect rate on a real inspection match what they claimed. Some suppliers offer a formal sample or
pilot order policy with different terms than a standard order, worth asking about directly rather than
assuming it doesn't exist. If the first order clears clean, place a second trial order before scaling up to
full volume, since a first order can go well by luck (a slow week on their end, a rep who personally
babysat it) in a way a second one is less likely to repeat. Only after two clean orders does it make sense
to fold a supplier into your normal reorder cadence.

## Red flags that predict a bad relationship before the first order ships

A few patterns show up again and again in supplier relationships that go wrong later, and nearly all of them
are visible before you ever place an order:

- **Pressure to commit big on the first deal.** A supplier who pushes for a large first order, or better
  pricing tied to a bigger commitment than you're comfortable with, is optimizing for their close, not for
  your risk. A supplier confident in the relationship is usually fine starting small.
- **No fixed business address, or a P.O. box only.** Not disqualifying on its own for a smaller manufacturer,
  but worth a direct question if it's the only address you can find anywhere, including their own invoices.
- **Payment requested to a personal account rather than a business account.** This is one of the clearest
  signals of a problem, not a minor administrative quirk.
- **Vague or shifting answers on lead time and MOQ.** A supplier who gives you a different number in the
  second conversation than the first, without explaining why, either doesn't track their own operations
  closely or is telling you what they think you want to hear.
- **No willingness to put quality or inspection terms in writing.** Verbal assurances that "our quality is
  great" mean nothing once a shipment actually fails and there's no agreed standard to point back to.
- **Only one contact exists, and they're unreachable for stretches at a time.** A single point of failure in
  a business you're about to depend on for restocking is a real operational risk, not just an inconvenience.

None of these alone automatically rules a supplier out, plenty of good, smaller vendors have a thin website
or a single contact. But two or more together, especially pressure to commit combined with vague terms, is
the exact combination that shows up in hindsight when a supplier relationship goes bad in month four.

## Why this gets skipped, and what it costs later

Onboarding checklists get skipped for a boring reason: the first order feels urgent, and confirming six
things in writing feels like friction on a deal that's already moving. The cost shows up later, once volume
is real. A lead time you never pinned down becomes a stockout you can't explain. An MOQ you didn't confirm
becomes a rejected purchase order the week you needed stock most. A quality standard nobody agreed on
becomes an argument over a failed shipment with no paper trail to settle it. Every one of these is cheaper
to prevent during onboarding than to untangle after the fact, and none of them require anything more
sophisticated than asking the right questions before the first order, not after.

## Keeping onboarding terms from getting lost once you have a dozen suppliers

A single new supplier is easy to onboard carefully. The problem is that the same checklist, done well ten or
fifteen times over, produces ten or fifteen sets of lead time ranges, MOQs, and quality terms that live in
different email threads, spread across however many vendors you're currently working with. That's exactly
the point where [tracking suppliers by hand starts to break
down](/blog/how-many-suppliers-is-too-many-to-track-by-hand/), not because the onboarding was done wrong,
but because the terms nobody wrote down anywhere central quietly drift out of memory. [Supplier
Coordination](/supplier-coordination/) keeps every supplier's agreed terms, lead time history, and open
purchase orders in one place from the first order onward, and flags a supplier whose actual performance
starts drifting from what they committed to at onboarding, so a slipping lead time or a rising defect rate
gets caught while it's still one bad month, not a pattern nobody noticed until a customer did.

## FAQ

**What should be included in a supplier onboarding checklist?**
At minimum: a lead time range (not a single average), minimum order quantity and case pack size per SKU,
written quality and inspection expectations, payment terms, a named backup contact beyond the sales rep, and
the supplier's policy on sample or trial orders. Confirming all six before the first purchase order prevents
most of the surprises that surface later, the same terms [Supplier Coordination](/supplier-coordination/)
then tracks for the life of the relationship.

**How big should a first order with a new supplier be?**
Small enough that a bad outcome doesn't hurt financially, but large enough to actually test lead time,
packaging, and quality against what the supplier committed to. Place a second trial order before scaling to
full volume, the same conservative sizing approach that works for [forecasting a brand new SKU with no
sales history yet](/blog/forecast-demand-without-a-data-team/), since a first order can go smoothly by luck
in a way a second one is less likely to repeat.

**What's a reasonable defect rate to accept from a new supplier?**
There's no universal number, but 2% is a common starting threshold for a first order with an unproven
vendor, tighter for safety- or regulation-sensitive goods. What matters more than the exact percentage is
agreeing on it, and on what happens when a shipment fails it, in writing before the order ships, the same
inspection standard [Quality Monitoring](/quality-monitoring/) checks automatically once real inspection
data is flowing.

**How is onboarding a new supplier different from managing lead time variability on an existing one?**
Onboarding is about establishing the terms and testing them once. Ongoing management is about tracking how
a supplier's real performance holds up against those terms over dozens of orders, which is a different
problem: see [managing supplier lead time variability](/blog/supplier-lead-time-slips/) for how to catch a
trusted supplier's lead time slipping after the relationship is already established.

**What's the biggest red flag when vetting a new supplier?**
Pressure to commit to a large first order combined with vague or shifting answers on lead time and MOQ. A
confident, established supplier is usually comfortable starting small and can answer specifics consistently
across conversations; a supplier pushing volume before you've placed a single order is optimizing for their
close, not for the relationship.
